Annuities and the Mathematics of Loans

Two earlier sets of notes dealt with money separately: one built the compound interest formula for a single deposit growing on its own, and another summed a geometric series to value a stream of monthly savings one deposit at a time. An annuity is what results from naming that second idea properly and giving it its own formula, so that a stream of equal payments can be valued in one step instead of being summed term by term. This set of notes gives the future and present value formulas for an annuity, covers the one change needed when a payment falls at the start of a period rather than the end, and finishes by running the same formulas backward to price a loan and a sinking fund.

An annuity is a stream of equal payments

An annuity is any finite sequence of equal payments made at fixed periods of time over a stated term, called the payment period. Two timings are both common enough to need their own name. In an ordinary annuity, each payment falls at the end of its period, a salary paid at the end of the month, or a bond coupon paid at the end of a year. In an annuity due, each payment falls at the start of its period instead, a rent payment due the first of the month being the clearest everyday example. The distinction matters because a payment made at the start of a period earns interest for one extra period compared with the same payment made at the end, which is the only difference the formulas below have to account for.

Future value of an ordinary annuity

The future value of an ordinary annuity, the balance immediately after the final payment, is given by FV = C × [(1 + i)n − 1]/i, where C is the payment amount, i is the interest rate per period, and n is the number of payments. Consider an annuity of 50 at the end of every three months for three years, at 6 percent compounded quarterly. Here i = 0.06/4 = 0.015 per quarter and n = 3 × 4 = 12 quarters, so

FV = 50 × [(1.015)12 − 1]/0.015 ≈ 652.06

Since 12 payments of 50 total 600, the compound interest earned over the three years is 652.06 − 600 = 52.06, a figure the formula hands over directly rather than requiring 12 separate compound interest calculations added together.

Present value of an ordinary annuity

The present value of an ordinary annuity answers a different question: what lump sum today is worth exactly the same as the whole stream of future payments? It is given by PV = C × [1 − (1 + i)−n]/i. Consider the present value of an annuity of 100 per month for 3.5 years at 6 percent compounded monthly: here i = 0.06/12 = 0.005 per month and n = 3.5 × 12 = 42 months, so

PV = 100 × [1 − (1.005)−42]/0.005 ≈ 3,779

Depositing roughly 3,779 today, at that same monthly rate, would fund exactly 42 monthly withdrawals of 100 each, the present value formula compressing 42 separate payments into a single number comparable to any other lump sum.

Annuity due: paying at the start instead of the end

Since every payment in an annuity due arrives one period earlier than the equivalent ordinary annuity, each of its payments earns exactly one extra period of interest. That is the entire adjustment needed: FVdue = FVordinary × (1 + i), and PVdue = PVordinary × (1 + i). For an annuity due of six yearly payments of 200, at 5 percent compounded annually, working through both formulas gives a present value of approximately 1,065 and a future value, at the end of the sixth year, of approximately 1,428.40, both slightly larger than the matching ordinary annuity would produce, exactly the extra period of interest the earlier timing buys.

Running the formula backward: loans and sinking funds

Both annuity formulas can be solved for whichever quantity is actually unknown. A loan is usually posed with the present value already fixed, the amount borrowed, and the payment itself is what needs to be found: solving PV = C × [1 − (1 + i)−n]/i for C gives C = (PV × i)/[1 − (1 + i)−n]. For a 10,000 loan repaid in four equal end of year payments at 6 percent compounded annually,

C = (10,000 × 0.06)/[1 − (1.06)−4] ≈ 2,885.91

so four payments of about 2,885.91 retire a 10,000 loan entirely, the same mathematics as the future value annuity, only read in the opposite direction.

A sinking fund runs the future value formula backward instead, solving for the payment C needed to reach a known future target. A machine costing 7000 is to be replaced after eight years, at which point it will have a salvage value of 700, so the fund needs to accumulate 7000 − 700 = 6300 by then. Depositing equal payments at the end of each quarter into a fund earning 8 percent compounded quarterly, with i = 0.08/4 = 0.02 per quarter and n = 4 × 8 = 32 quarters, solving FV = C × [(1 + i)n − 1]/i = 6300 for C gives a required quarterly payment of approximately 142, a steady, modest deposit standing in for the much larger replacement cost it is quietly being built up to cover.

A single deposit, a stream of savings, a loan, and a sinking fund have turned out, across three sets of notes now, to be the same handful of formulas rearranged for whichever quantity is missing. That reuse, more than any individual formula, is the real payoff of treating compound interest and the geometric series as one connected topic rather than as separate tricks.

Wood, Introductory Mathematical Analysis for Business, Economics, and the Life and Social Sciences, 14th edition (Pearson Canada, 2019), chapter 5, covering the future and present value of an ordinary annuity, the annuity due, loan payments, and sinking funds.
