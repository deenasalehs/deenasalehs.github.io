Why Doesn't Growth Always Improve People's Lives?

This series opened with the observation that a high growth rate is not, by itself, a sufficient measure of how an economy is doing, that growth has to actually show up in people's lives to count for anything. It is worth closing the series by asking directly why that gap between the headline growth figure and the lived experience of citizens opens up in the first place, even when the reported GDP number is accurate.

Who actually receives the income

The most direct explanation is unfair and unequal income distribution, a problem covered in depth in the piece on inequality earlier in this series. When growth is concentrated at the top, the gap between rich and poor widens even as the aggregate economy expands, and this is a more common pattern in developing countries than in already wealthy ones, where redistribution systems are typically more established.

What the growth is actually producing

Not all additional output serves the same purpose, and a useful way to see this is to look at what a country's growth is actually made of. A government oriented toward citizen welfare tends to direct resources toward consumption goods, the things households actually use day to day. A government more oriented toward state power tends to direct resources toward industrial goods such as iron and steel, inputs that build national capacity rather than household living standards. Two countries can report identical growth rates while producing very different things, and only one of those growth paths is likely to be felt directly by ordinary households.

Standard of living against standard of welfare

This distinction, introduced in the piece on Sen's capability approach, is worth restating here because it explains so much of the gap between growth and wellbeing. A high standard of living, measured by income and assets, does not guarantee a high standard of welfare, the degree to which people can actually enjoy what they have. Someone can earn a strong income while being so overloaded with work that they have no time or energy left to spend it on anything that improves their life. National growth figures capture the first of these two things far more easily than the second.

How much the government intervenes, and in what kind of system

The degree and direction of government intervention shapes how growth translates into welfare, and it is useful to place that intervention on a spectrum defined by three broad economic systems. A centrally planned economy has the government controlling the entirety of economic activity and owning all productive resources. A free market economy leaves resource ownership and economic decision making almost entirely to individuals, with government restricted mainly to political functions, infrastructure, justice, and security. A mixed economy sits between the two, with ownership and economic control shared between government and individuals, and in practice this is where most of the world's economies sit today, generally closer to the market end of the spectrum than the planned end.

In a mixed economy, one of government's central jobs is correcting market failures that would otherwise reduce efficiency and welfare. Two failures come up repeatedly. Monopoly power lets a single firm restrict output and raise prices well above what a competitive market would produce, which is why governments maintain anti trust laws and actively encourage competition. Negative externalities are the side effects of economic activity that fall on people who were not party to the transaction that caused them, environmental damage and pollution being the clearest examples. A new factory can genuinely expand economic activity while also degrading local air or water quality, so without pollution controls in place, the same investment that shows up as growth in the national accounts can simultaneously make the people living near it worse off.

The throughline across all of this is that growth is not self executing. Whether an expanding economy actually improves people's lives depends on how income is distributed, what is being produced, whether people have the time and capacity to enjoy what they earn, and how actively government addresses the market failures that would otherwise let growth come at the expense of the very people it is supposed to benefit. None of that is visible in a GDP figure on its own, which is exactly why this series spent so much time on the ideas that sit underneath it.
